\begin{abstract}
Synthetic human faces produced by generative adversarial networks and denoising diffusion models increasingly challenge digital media authentication, identity verification, and courtroom evidentiary review. Existing detection methods predominantly rely on standalone deep neural networks trained for binary classification. Although such classifiers achieve high accuracy on test splits matched to their training distributions, their predictions degrade sharply under unseen generative architectures, external authentic face distributions, and lossy social-media compression. Returning an uncalibrated binary probability without provenance, uncertainty bounds, or an abstention mechanism exposes forensic examiners to silent false accusations and missed manipulations. This paper proposes the AI Forensic Analyst, a modular, evidence-driven forensic architecture that separates image-reading signal extraction from an image-blind four-way decision engine capable of issuing Real, Fake, Inconclusive, or Unknown outcomes. Using a public, open-license five-dataset benchmark spanning ArtiFact, Diffusion Facial Forgery, 140k Real and Fake Faces, Synthbuster, and FairFace, we train and stress-test three distinct neural architectures under identical fairness invariants: ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B4, and Vision Transformer ViT-B/16. While scaling from ResNet-50 (0.9261 area under the receiver operating characteristic curve) to ViT-B/16 raises in-distribution area under the curve to 0.9750, balanced accuracy to 0.9153, and calibration error down to 0.0211, zero-shot cross-dataset evaluation across 46,369 test images per model reveals that even ViT-B/16 misses 64.0\% of unseen diffusion faces, misses 82.1\% of Synthbuster syntheses, and falsely accuses 62.5\% of FairFace authentic portraits, polarizing in the opposite direction from convolutional baselines. We then formalize reliability-weighted multi-specialist fusion, validation-calibrated four-dimensional confidence estimation, and programmatically verified forensic reasoning across eighteen decoupled modules. Across sixteen fault scenarios and a 2,700-case nine-track governance benchmark, cross-paradigm multi-specialist fusion reduces silent erroneous verdicts to 3.0\% on clean ArtiFact, 1.3\% on 140k Faces, 3.0\% on Synthbuster, 22.3\% on FairFace, and 33.3\% on unseen diffusion faces, eliminates 48.7\% to 100.0\% of silent degradation errors, and prevents ungrounded textual claims.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Recent advances in deep generative modeling have transformed facial synthesis from coarse image editing into photorealistic portrait generation \cite{Croitoru_2026}. Early synthesis pipelines relied primarily on Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), where adversarial training progressively refined spatial fidelity across architectures such as StyleGAN and its successors \cite{Melnik_2024}. More recently, denoising and latent diffusion models have enabled text-conditioned, image-conditioned, and inpainting-based facial generation with substantially fewer geometric or blending flaws \cite{Cheng_2024}. While these tools support creative media and privacy-preserving data workflows, their misuse for impersonation, fabricated journalism, and manipulated legal evidence poses a serious challenge for digital forensic examiners \cite{Hydara_2024}.

In current research practice, synthetic face detection is overwhelmingly formulated as standalone binary classification \cite{Heidari_2023}. A convolutional neural network (CNN) or Vision Transformer (ViT) ingests a cropped face image and outputs a single scalar probability indicating whether the sample is authentic or synthetic \cite{Malik_2022}. On test partitions drawn from the same generator family and camera source as the training set, supervised detectors routinely exceed 95\% accuracy \cite{Khan_2024}. However, controlled cross-domain evaluations demonstrate that these classifiers frequently latch onto generator-specific upsampling traces \cite{Guarnera_2020} or superficial dataset shortcuts such as compression history and color statistics \cite{Altamimi_2026}. When deployed against unseen diffusion models \cite{Cheng_2024}, demographically diverse authentic portraits \cite{Xu_2024}, or heavily re-encoded social-media uploads \cite{Guo_2024}, standalone detectors experience steep spikes in both false negative rates (FNR, missed forgeries) and false positive rates (FPR, authentic faces misclassified as synthetic).

From an operational digital forensics standpoint, this fragility reflects an architectural flaw rather than merely a backbone limitation \cite{Silva_2022}. Monolithic classifiers conflate four distinct forensic responsibilities inside a single opaque pass: raw signal extraction, evidentiary reliability assessment, probability calibration, and final verdict issuance. When a standalone network outputs a raw softmax score of $0.99$ on a degraded or out-of-distribution image, an investigator receives no measure of empirical calibration \cite{Jin_2025}, no corroborating signal audit, and no structured mechanism to abstain \cite{Maiano_2026}. Conversely, enforcing a naive high probability cutoff to suppress false positives can cause severe recall collapse in judicial screening workflows \cite{Hydara_2024}.

To address these limitations, this study re-examines synthetic face authentication from both an empirical failure-analysis perspective and a systems-forensics perspective. Specifically, this work makes four main contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Open-License Cross-Paradigm and Multi-Backbone Stress Benchmark:} We assemble and audit a strictly public, open-license five-dataset benchmark (ArtiFact, Diffusion Facial Forgery, 140k Real and Fake Faces, Synthbuster, and FairFace) under deterministic seven-stage preprocessing and leakage-verified grouped and leave-one-generator-out (LOGO) splits. Using this benchmark, we quantify the failure modes of three distinct neural architectures (ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B4, and Vision Transformer ViT-B/16) across calibration, subgroup heterogeneity, 53 synthetic perturbations, four external corpora, controlled Stable Diffusion holdout, and paired statistical ablations.
  \item \textbf{Trust-Bounded Eighteen-Module Forensic Architecture:} We design and implement the AI Forensic Analyst across eighteen single-responsibility modules (denoted M1 through M18), enforcing an explicit trust boundary between image-reading intake (M1), validation (M2), nine independent contextual collectors (M3), and an isolated deep learning specialist (M4) on one side, and an image-blind downstream core governed by an append-only evidence ledger (M5), non-destructive reliability validator (M6), pluggable fusion engine (M7), and sole four-way Decision Engine (M8: \textit{Real}, \textit{Fake}, \textit{Inconclusive}, \textit{Unknown}) on the other.
  \item \textbf{Validation-Calibrated Four-Dimensional Confidence and Abstention:} We formulate a transparent calibration framework (M9) that separates raw neural probability from empirical validation-bin accuracy ($C_{\text{pred}}$), collector reliability ($C_{\text{ev}}$), and pipeline execution completeness ($C_{\text{sys}}$), combining them into a conservatively capped decision confidence ($C_{\text{dec}}$) that prevents uncorroborated or contested neural scores from producing overconfident binary verdicts.
  \item \textbf{Programmatically Grounded Forensic Reasoning and End-to-End Verification:} We introduce an anti-hallucination reasoning engine (M11) that verifies every generated claim against immutable ledger identifiers alongside structured data-only explainability (M10), provenance-stamped reporting (M12), and full-stack web delivery (M13--M14), verifying the complete architecture across 16 end-to-end forensic fault and decision scenarios.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Research on synthetic face and AI-generated image forensics has expanded substantially across the transition from early GAN architectures to modern diffusion and foundation-model pipelines \cite{Croitoru_2026}. To establish a rigorous, traceable foundation without relying on unexamined citation clusters, we review 44 peer-reviewed studies organized across five methodological themes: frequency and convolutional trace analysis, spatial and foundation-model classification, biometric localization and proactive provenance, fairness and perturbation benchmarking, and uncertainty calibration with forensic governance.

\subsection{Frequency-Domain, Spectral, and Convolutional Trace Forensics}

Signal-processing forensic methods inspect deterministic mathematical traces left by upsampling or iterative denoising operators rather than relying exclusively on opaque spatial representations \cite{Gragnaniello_2022}. In early GAN forensics, Guarnera et al.\ \cite{Guarnera_2020} modeled local pixel correlations with an Expectation-Maximization algorithm to extract convolutional fingerprints across five GAN architectures, showing that transposed-convolution layers imprint separable generator traces. Extending spectral inspection, Giudice et al.\ \cite{Giudice_2021} fitted Laplace beta distributions to $8\times 8$ Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) alternating-current coefficients, achieving lightweight, inherently interpretable GAN detection without deep neural training. However, Alkishri et al.\ \cite{Alkishri_2024} demonstrated that targeted GAN fingerprint removal perturbations substantially degrade trace-based detectors, exposing their vulnerability to intentional anti-forensic laundering.

Because denoising diffusion models synthesize imagery through iterative score matching rather than single-pass transposed convolutions, GAN-calibrated spectral rules do not transfer directly to diffusion outputs. Addressing diffusion artifacts directly, Bammey \cite{Bammey_2024} proposed Synthbuster, a training-free pipeline applying cross-difference high-pass filtering and Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) peak analysis across nine diffusion generators, avoiding supervised training-set bias at the cost of sensitivity to heavy post-processing. Within learned architectures, Guo et al.\ \cite{Guo_2024} designed Space-Frequency Interactive Convolution (SFIConv) alongside Multichannel Constrained Separable Convolution to couple spatial and high-frequency features inside standard CNN backbones, reaching 96.47\% area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) on low-quality FaceForensics++ videos yet only 71.0\% ROC-AUC on unseen Celeb-DF. More recently, Yan et al.\ \cite{Yan_2026} combined four-subband Discrete Wavelet Transform window tiling with phase-only FFT attention across 65 generative models, achieving 92.1\% mean accuracy overall while observing sharp degradation to 44.1\%--52.1\% on face-swapping subsets and 54.6\%--60.8\% on social-media subsets.

\subsection{Spatial CNNs, Vision Transformers, and Cross-Domain Generalization}

Supervised spatial-domain classifiers represent the dominant paradigm in facial forgery detection \cite{Heidari_2023}. Early comparative studies evaluated standard convolutional architectures on constrained datasets: Taeb and Chi \cite{Taeb_2022} compared VGG19, DenseNet-121, and custom CNNs on a small augmented face dataset without cross-dataset testing, while Safwat et al.\ \cite{Safwat_2024} paired GAN feature extraction with ResNet classification on only 2,041 images. In social-media contexts, Preeti et al.\ \cite{Preeti_2023} explored an initial GAN-based social-media detector on a single distribution, which Sharma et al.\ \cite{Sharma_2025} subsequently extended into a multi-classifier ensemble improving compression resilience on GAN images while leaving diffusion synthesis untested. Competition benchmarks such as the Face Deepfake Detection Challenge organized by Guarnera et al.\ \cite{Guarnera_2022} standardized multi-GAN evaluation and confirmed the strong intra-domain performance of EfficientNet backbones, yet remained restricted to GAN-era distributions.

When evaluated across unseen datasets and generative families, spatial classifiers suffer pronounced generalization drops. Khan and Dang-Nguyen \cite{Khan_2024} benchmarked eight supervised CNNs and two self-supervised transformers (DINO and CLIP) across four manipulation corpora, reporting that cross-dataset accuracy falls from above 95\% in-domain to 60\%--70\% out-of-domain and that pretraining diversity outweighs backbone complexity. On diffusion-generated faces, Cheng et al.\ \cite{Cheng_2024} introduced the 500,000-image Diffusion Facial Forgery (DiFF) dataset across 13 diffusion methods and showed that conventional detectors score below 30\% accuracy before proposing edge-graph regularization. Exploring transformer patch tokens, Arshed et al.\ \cite{Arshed_2024} reported 99.90\% F1 using a patch-wise ViT on a four-class StyleGAN2 and Stable Diffusion split, although cross-dataset validation was omitted. For structured source attribution, Guarnera et al.\ \cite{Guarnera_2024} arranged ResNet-101 modules into a three-level hierarchy separating authenticity, paradigm (GAN versus diffusion), and specific generator on an 83,000-image proprietary corpus.

Recent cross-domain studies seek invariant forensic representations through feature purification, vision-language alignment, and operator probing. Meng et al.\ \cite{Meng_2024} proposed the Artifact Purification Network, combining Hermitian-symmetric DFT band proposals, orthogonal spatial decomposition, and mutual-information minimization to reach 80.4\% cross-generator accuracy on GenImage, though without compression stress testing or face-specific evaluation. Wu et al.\ \cite{Wu_2026} introduced LASTED, pairing main authenticity labels with ClipCap domain-caption supervision under bidirectional contrastive learning to attain 0.913--0.974 average precision across four cross-domain benchmarks, while recording its lowest subset precision (0.873) on facial deepfakes. Complementing heavy foundation backbones, Shen and Tian \cite{Shen_2026} developed PORI-Gate, an 11.77M-parameter ResNet-18 architecture that dynamically gates color-quantization, low-bit-plane, and JPEG-residual probes to achieve 92.86\% mean accuracy across 38 subsets, while transparently documenting severe accuracy drops on Improved-Diffusion (60.38\%) and threshold-shifted DDPM samples (76.37\% accuracy despite 90.16\% ROC-AUC).

\subsection{Biometric Localization, Reconstruction Dynamics, and Proactive Provenance}

Alternative forensic strategies target anatomical consistency, generative manifold reconstruction, spatial localization, or proactive source authentication. Pasquini et al.\ \cite{Pasquini_2023} combined GAN inversion with biometric trait analysis to expose identity-level anomalies on synthetic portraits. Focusing on ocular physiology, Xue et al.\ \cite{Xue_2023} proposed GLFNet, fusing local iris and pupil features with global ResNeSt residuals on CelebA-HQ. For pixel-level localization, Huang et al.\ \cite{Huang_2022} designed FakeLocator using a DeepLabv3-ResNet101 encoder-decoder and face-parsing attention to regress full-resolution gray-scale fakeness maps across seven GANs, achieving 0.9846 ROC-AUC on FaceForensics++ while remaining tied to GAN upsampling textures. As Melnik et al.\ \cite{Melnik_2024} documented in their survey of StyleGAN architectures, and as Abbas and Taeihagh \cite{Abbas_2026} illustrated through hybrid latent-diffusion IC-DGAN facial expression synthesis, modern generators increasingly eliminate overt biometric and blending defects.

Reconstruction-error methods instead test whether an input lies near a generative manifold. Zeng et al.\ \cite{Zeng_2025} extracted latent diffusion reconstruction residuals for synthetic face identification, demonstrating feasibility while noting that multi-step denoising reconstruction introduces substantial inference latency. Outside facial forensics, Cho et al.\ \cite{Cho_2026} applied context-aware diffusion inpainting to industrial defect synthesis, illustrating how localized diffusion masks blend seamlessly into surrounding textures. In contrast, proactive provenance frameworks alter media prior to release: Nadimpalli and Rattani \cite{Nadimpalli_2024} embedded GAN-based visible watermarks for proactive deepfake detection, while Ghani et al.\ \cite{Ghani_2024} and Ul Ghani et al.\ \cite{Ul_Ghani_2024} integrated GAN synthesis with distributed blockchain ledgers to secure facial recognition templates. Although effective within closed ecosystems, proactive watermarking and blockchain registration cannot authenticate unwatermarked images encountered in reactive forensic investigations.

\subsection{Demographic Fairness, Compression Robustness, and Evaluation Benchmarks}

Fairness audits and uniform benchmarks reveal that dataset bias and post-processing sensitivity systematically inflate published metrics. Altamimi et al.\ \cite{Altamimi_2026} audited ResNet-101, EfficientNet-B4, and Xception across GAN and diffusion faces, discovering that a zero-learning statistical audit achieved 85\% accuracy due to low-level dataset bias while EfficientNet-B4 dropped to 86.75\% on diffusion faces. At massive scale, Xu et al.\ \cite{Xu_2024} transferred 65.3 million facial attribute annotations across five deepfake databases and proved via imbalance-corrected metrics that both demographic and non-demographic attributes induce pronounced detector bias. Evaluating 22 pretrained checkpoints of six diffusion detectors on 40,000 DiffSynFace images paired with FairFace, CelebA-HQ, and Adience, Panda et al.\ \cite{Panda_2026} observed strong average precision alongside severe fixed-threshold miscalibration (such as 50\% accuracy despite 81.5\% average precision). Targeting South Asian representation, Reza et al.\ \cite{Reza_2026} introduced ARC-Net (EfficientNet-B0 with residual and attention blocks), reducing real-face FPR on Bangladeshi portraits from 22.0\% to 10.0\% and validating Grad-CAM overlap quantitatively (0.8396 mean intersection-over-union), though without diffusion or compression testing.

Broader surveys and uniform benchmarks reinforce these structural concerns. Malik et al.\ \cite{Malik_2022} and Dang and Nguyen \cite{Dang_2023} cataloged pre-diffusion image and video manipulation detectors, while Edwards et al.\ \cite{Edwards_2024} reviewed generation and detection datasets through early diffusion models. Under uniform retraining of 11 detectors across seven datasets (644 training runs), Deng et al.\ \cite{Deng_2024} demonstrated that every evaluated detector falls to 51.2\%--60.5\% average ROC-AUC on an independent human-vetted test set and degrades sharply under Gaussian noise and compression. Extending this benchmark lens to modern generative AI, Croitoru et al.\ \cite{Croitoru_2026} showed that seven state-of-the-art detectors lose more than 30\% ROC-AUC on unseen diffusion and 3D talking-face generators, highlighting uncertainty calibration and modular late fusion as urgent open priorities.

\subsection{Uncertainty Calibration, Out-of-Distribution Detection, and Forensic Governance}

Despite high legal stakes, very few studies address predictive uncertainty, out-of-distribution (OOD) rejection, or human-auditable forensic governance. Silva et al.\ \cite{Silva_2022} proposed an explainable hierarchical ensemble combining Xception and EfficientNet-B3 with weakly supervised attention cropping/dropping, Grad-CAM region extraction, and secondary 2D DFT and DCT beta inspection; notably, while attaining 92.20\% accuracy on DFDC, their ensemble dropped to 72.73\% on unconstrained WildDeepfake videos and revealed that 60\% of misclassifications occurred within a narrow probability margin below $0.5$. In a judicial prototype pairing Django with a pretrained ViT, Hydara et al.\ \cite{Hydara_2024} found that raising the confidence threshold to 80\%--85\% to protect courtroom precision caused detection recall to collapse to 18\%.

Three recent studies directly investigate OOD detection, calibration, and selective abstention. Maiano et al.\ \cite{Maiano_2026} cascaded U-Net and Tiny DeiT classifiers with reconstruction- and attention-residual Abnormality modules on an 82,000-image continual deepfake benchmark, reaching 91.6\%--93.77\% OOD AUROC on content shifts while observing unsupervised OOD drops to 60.14\%--63.35\% and transformer attention collapse on cross-generator splits. Jin et al.\ \cite{Jin_2025} introduced PE-DD, embedding four Packed-Ensembles estimators with Steganalysis-Rich Model (SRM) filters to lower expected calibration error (ECE) to $0.005$--$0.020$ in-domain and achieve the lowest Brier score across four unseen benchmarks, although ECE still degraded six- to ten-fold under heavy compression without an explicit abstention state. Fatima et al.\ \cite{Fatima_2026} combined a semantic-plus-SRM two-stream network with worst-of-$K$ counter-forensic red-teaming and test-time defense on DeepFakeFace ($\text{ECE}\le 0.0196$), yet evaluated only 240 held-out test pairs without cross-dataset or GAN baselines. \section{Proposed Forensic Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

To overcome the fragility of standalone binary classifiers in evidentiary settings, our methodology couples rigorous empirical failure characterization with a trust-bounded, multi-module forensic decision architecture. Rather than treating a convolutional neural network as an autonomous arbiter, we treat neural inference as one fallible specialist signal that must be logged in an immutable ledger, graded for reliability alongside independent image-integrity collectors, calibrated against empirical validation accuracy, and evaluated by an image-blind four-way Decision Engine.

The proposed methodology is structured in four parts. Subsection~\ref{subsec:benchmark} establishes our strictly public five-dataset benchmark, visual sample diversity, deterministic seven-stage preprocessing pipeline, and leakage-verified evaluation splits. Subsection~\ref{subsec:architecture} defines all eighteen modules (denoted M1 through M18) and formalizes the architectural trust boundaries separating raw image access from verdict authority. Subsection~\ref{subsec:fusion} derives the mathematical formulations governing reliability-weighted evidence fusion, validation-calibrated four-dimensional confidence estimation, and four-way abstention. Subsection~\ref{subsec:explain} details structured explainability, anti-hallucination sentence verification, and reproducible web delivery.

Within Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}(a) Trust Boundary A (the image-reading perception layer), M1 enforces file-extension allowlists and a $20$\,MB upload cap while recording cryptographic SHA-256 file digests and rejecting malformed payloads without disk persistence. In Figure~\ref{fig:fusion}(a) and Figure~\ref{fig:fusion}(b) (M6--M7), each validated collector item $i$ is mapped from its discrete M6 reliability grade (\texttt{high}, \texttt{medium}, \texttt{low}, \texttt{unusable}) to a deterministic reliability weight $w_i \in \{1.0, 0.6, 0.3, 0.0\}$. Under the active interpretable \texttt{rule\_based} fusion strategy (with \texttt{weighted} and \texttt{bayesian} strategies reserved under the \texttt{FusionStrategy} contract), only calibrated directional specialists cast class-polarity votes, whereas the nine contextual M3 collectors provide reliability-graded corroborating context without fabricating directional votes. For $K$ usable directional specialists ($w_k > 0$), each specialist $k$ with fake-class probability $p_k$ and validation-frozen operating threshold $t_k$ (for the baseline ResNet-50, $t_1 = 0.3951$) yields polarity sign $s_k = +1$ if $p_k \ge t_k$ and $s_k = -1$ if $p_k < t_k$. The normalized directional polarity balance $B \in [-1, 1]$ is computed as:
\begin{equation}
  B = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K} s_k \, w_k}{\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k}.
  \label{eq:balance}
\end{equation}
In Eq.~\eqref{eq:balance}, $K$ denotes the count of directional specialist items with positive reliability weight ($w_k > 0$), $s_k \in \{-1, +1\}$ denotes the thresholded directional polarity of specialist $k$, and $w_k \in \{0.3, 0.6, 1.0\}$ is the M6 reliability weight assigned to specialist $k$. When $K = 0$, no division is performed and M7 emits a \texttt{fused\_conclusion} lean of \texttt{no\_directional\_evidence}. When $K \ge 1$ and opposing specialists yield $|B| < 0.5$, M7 emits \texttt{contested}; otherwise, $B \ge 0.5$ yields \texttt{positive\_class} and $B \le -0.5$ yields \texttt{negative\_class}. Importantly, the fused conclusion record contains zero verdict labels (\textit{Real} or \textit{Fake}) and is itself logged in M5 and validated by M6.

In Figure~\ref{fig:fusion}(c) (M9), to prevent raw neural softmax scores from masquerading as calibrated forensic certainty \cite{Jin_2025}, M9 computes four distinct confidence dimensions. First, validation-calibrated prediction confidence $C_{\text{pred}} \in [0, 1]$ maps raw specialist output onto empirical validation accuracy:
\begin{equation}
  C_{\text{pred}} = w_{\text{det}} \cdot A_{\text{val}}(r), \quad \text{where } r = \begin{cases} p & \text{if } p \ge t, \\ 1 - p & \text{if } p < t. \end{cases}
  \label{eq:cpred}
\end{equation}
In Eq.~\eqref{eq:cpred}, $p \in [0, 1]$ is the raw fake-class probability emitted by M4, $t \in (0, 1)$ is the frozen validation threshold ($t = 0.3951$), $r \in [0, 1]$ is the directional raw confidence aligned with the predicted side of $t$, $A_{\text{val}}(r) \in [0, 1]$ is the empirical accuracy looked up from the corresponding 15-bin validation reliability table, and $w_{\text{det}} \in \{0.3, 0.6, 1.0\}$ is the M6 reliability weight of the usable detector item (if no usable detector item exists, $C_{\text{pred}}$ is recorded as \texttt{null}). For instance, a raw baseline output of $p = 0.99$ with \texttt{high} reliability ($w_{\text{det}} = 1.0$) maps to $C_{\text{pred}} = 0.9717$ rather than $0.99$.

Second, evidentiary corroboration confidence $C_{\text{ev}} \in [0, 1]$ aggregates collector quality across all $N$ registered collector items:
\begin{equation}
  C_{\text{ev}} = \left(\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i\right) \cdot (1 - 0.5\,\delta_{\text{conf}}).
  \label{eq:cev}
\end{equation}
In Eq.~\eqref{eq:cev}, $N \ge 1$ is the total number of registered collector items (including \texttt{unusable} items with $w_i = 0.0$), $w_i \in \{0.0, 0.3, 0.6, 1.0\}$ is the M6 reliability weight of collector item $i$, and $\delta_{\text{conf}} \in \{0, 1\}$ is a binary conflict indicator equal to $1$ if M6 flagged any inter-evidence contradiction and $0$ otherwise.

Third, system execution completeness $C_{\text{sys}} \in [0, 1]$ quantifies operational pipeline health:
\begin{equation}
  C_{\text{sys}} = \frac{N_{\text{usable}}}{N_{\text{attempted}} + N_{\text{refused}} + N_{\text{errors}}},
  \label{eq:csys}
\end{equation}
where $N_{\text{usable}}$ is the count of collector executions yielding usable (\texttt{high}, \texttt{medium}, or \texttt{low}) evidence, $N_{\text{attempted}}$ is the count of invoked collectors, $N_{\text{refused}}$ is the count of collectors refusing out-of-contract inputs, and $N_{\text{errors}}$ is the count of structured M16 execution failures.

Fourth, base composite confidence $C_{\text{base}} \in [0, 1]$ and conservative capped decision confidence $C_{\text{dec}} \in [0, 1]$ are computed when $C_{\text{pred}}$ is non-null ($C_{\text{dec}} = \texttt{null}$ whenever $C_{\text{pred}} = \texttt{null}$):
\begin{equation}
  C_{\text{base}} = 0.5\,C_{\text{pred}} + 0.3\,C_{\text{ev}} + 0.2\,C_{\text{sys}},
  \label{eq:cbase}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  C_{\text{dec}} = \gamma_{\text{src}} \cdot \gamma_{\text{lean}} \cdot \tilde{C}, \quad
  \tilde{C} = \begin{cases}
    \min(C_{\text{base}},\, C_{\text{ev}},\, C_{\text{sys}}) & \text{if } C_{\text{ev}} \le 0.50 \lor C_{\text{sys}} \le 0.50 \lor N_{\text{ctx}} = 0, \\
    C_{\text{base}} & \text{otherwise}.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:cdec}
\end{equation}
In Eqs.~\eqref{eq:cbase} and \eqref{eq:cdec}, the convex weights $(0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$ prioritize calibrated specialist accuracy while reserving half of total weight for evidentiary reliability and pipeline health; $N_{\text{ctx}}$ denotes the count of usable non-detector contextual collectors (M3); $\tilde{C}$ enforces a strict safety ceiling at $\min(C_{\text{ev}}, C_{\text{sys}})$ whenever supporting evidence or execution health falls at or below $0.50$ (or when zero contextual collectors are usable); $\gamma_{\text{src}} \in \{0.85, 1.0\}$ applies a $0.85$ single-source discount when directional polarity rests on a single specialist ($K = 1$, otherwise $1.0$); and $\gamma_{\text{lean}} \in \{0.50, 1.0\}$ applies a $0.50$ contested-evidence discount when the fused lean is \texttt{contested} (otherwise $1.0$).

In Figure~\ref{fig:fusion}(d) (M8), reading exclusively the validated \texttt{fused\_conclusion} and \texttt{confidence} \texttt{EV} identifiers, the image-blind Decision Engine issues exactly one of four forensic outcomes: \textit{Unknown} if the lean is \texttt{no\_directional\_evidence} or $C_{\text{dec}}$ is \texttt{null}; \textit{Inconclusive} if the lean is \texttt{contested}, $\delta_{\text{conf}} = 1$, or $C_{\text{dec}} < 0.50$; \textit{Fake} if the lean is \texttt{positive\_class}, $\delta_{\text{conf}} = 0$, and $C_{\text{dec}} \ge 0.50$; and \textit{Real} if the lean is \texttt{negative\_class}, $\delta_{\text{conf}} = 0$, and $C_{\text{dec}} \ge 0.50$.

\subsection{Data-Only Explainability, Anti-Hallucination Reasoning, and Delivery}
\label{subsec:explain}

Downstream transparency is enforced by M10, M11, and M12. Explainability module M10 emits structured JSON (\texttt{explanation.json}) across Grad-CAM, attention maps, saliency, and integrated gradients (reserving SHAP, LIME, and counterfactual slots), explicitly marking uncomputed visual tensors as \texttt{"no\_data"} (\texttt{data: null}) rather than fabricating synthetic heatmaps \cite{Altamimi_2026}. Grounded Reasoning module M11 generates human-readable case narratives (\texttt{reasoning.json}) strictly from validated ledger entries and runs a programmatic \texttt{verify\_sentence()} check confirming that every sentence cites valid \texttt{EV} identifiers whose exact numeric values match the ledger; any ungrounded statement immediately raises a structured M16 error. Report Generator M12 compiles deterministic JSON, Markdown, and HTML forensic reports stamped with model, dataset, threshold, and Git commit provenance. For interactive investigative workflows, M13 wraps the forensic pipeline behind a thread-safe Django REST Framework API exposing ten canonical endpoints with zero duplicated decision logic, and M14 provides an accessible React interface displaying live stage progression, four-way verdicts, all four confidence dimensions, and explicit evidentiary limitations (Supplementary Material S3).

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

All model training and perturbation evaluations were executed on NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPUs under a locked software environment (Python 3.12, PyTorch 2.10.0+cu128, and FastAI 2.8.7) governed by immutable YAML experiment configurations and SHA-256-verified atomic checkpoints (Supplementary Material S3). For the primary baseline ResNet-50, an ImageNet-1k pretrained ResNet-50 architecture was trained on the ArtiFact grouped random split ($34{,}993$ training, $7{,}502$ validation, and $7{,}505$ test images at $224\times 224$ pixels) using cross-entropy loss, the Adam optimizer with decoupled weight decay $0.01$, FP16 mixed precision, batch size 64, and FastAI's two-stage transfer schedule comprising 1 frozen-head epoch followed by 10 unfrozen one-cycle epochs (base learning rate $3.3113\times 10^{-4}$, early-stopping patience 3 on validation loss). Train-only geometric and photometric augmentations included horizontal flipping, rotation up to 10 degrees, zoom up to $1.05$, and brightness/contrast jitter up to $0.10$. Following strict validation-only threshold selection, the primary operating threshold maximizing validation balanced accuracy was frozen at $t = 0.3951$ (exact $0.395057$), supplemented by validation-selected screening ($t = 0.089$, targeting validation $\text{FNR} \le 0.05$) and flag-for-review ($t = 0.629$, targeting validation $\text{FPR} \le 0.05$) reference points.

To isolate pure unseen-generator failure from cross-dataset acquisition shifts, a controlled leave-one-generator-out (LOGO) experiment trained an identical ResNet-50 configuration on the ArtiFact LOGO partition, where all Stable Diffusion images were excluded from training ($40{,}873$ images across 12 seen generators plus real faces) and validation ($7{,}204$ images), and evaluated recall both on all $1{,}923$ held-out Stable Diffusion images and in a paired comparison against the baseline ResNet-50 on the exact 299 Stable Diffusion images common to both test sets. Furthermore, four controlled one-factor ablations were trained on the grouped random split by mutating exactly one factor of the baseline per run: the Seed-43 Replicate changed the random seed from 42 to 43 to measure run-to-run repeatability; the No-Augmentation Ablation disabled training data augmentation; the No-Pretraining Ablation disabled ImageNet-1k pretraining under the fixed 11-epoch training budget; and the Reduced-Resolution Ablation reduced spatial input resolution from $224\times 224$ to $160\times 160$ pixels. To test whether compound-scaled convolutional or self-attention backbones resolve cross-domain brittleness under identical grouped-split ($224\times 224$, seed 42, 1 frozen + 10 unfrozen epochs, Adam weight decay $0.01$, validation-only threshold freezing) fairness invariants, two additional architectures were trained on Tesla T4 GPUs: an ImageNet-1k pretrained EfficientNet-B4 (base learning rate $3.0\times 10^{-4}$, frozen threshold $t_3 = 0.5161$) and an ImageNet-21k-ft-1k pretrained Vision Transformer ViT-B/16 (base learning rate $2.0\times 10^{-4}$, frozen threshold $t_4 = 0.4906$).

Comprehensive stress testing evaluated each trained specialist at its frozen ArtiFact validation threshold without target-domain retuning across five axes: 15-bin probability calibration (ECE, MCE, and Brier score), per-generator FNR and per-real-source FPR with 95\% Wilson confidence intervals, 53 synthetic image degradations spanning 15 perturbation families across all $7{,}505$ test images, and zero-shot cross-dataset evaluation ($46{,}369$ total test images per backbone) on DiFF ($7{,}364$ stratified official test images; $51{,}452$ full processed pool audited), 140k Real and Fake Faces ($20{,}000$ held-out validation images), Synthbuster ($1{,}500$ stratified test images), and FairFace ($10{,}000$ stratified demographic portraits). Paired statistical comparisons on the $7{,}505$ grouped test predictions used $10{,}000$ percentile bootstrap resamples (seed 49) for ROC-AUC differences (reporting 95\% confidence intervals for the Seed-43 Replicate and cross-backbone comparators alongside Bonferroni-adjusted 98.3\% intervals across the three factor ablations) and exact two-sided McNemar tests on binary decisions. Because each ablation and comparator used a single random seed (yielding $n = 2 < 6$ baseline seeds, below the minimum sample size for a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test at $\alpha = 0.05$), all comparisons report saved-run differences with no cross-seed superiority claim. Finally, whole-platform verification tested the complete eighteen-module AI Forensic Analyst across 16 deterministic fault-injection scenarios and a $2{,}700$-case nine-track governance benchmark comparing single-specialist ($K = 1$) and cross-paradigm multi-specialist ($K = 3$: ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B4, and ViT-B/16) fusion, reinforced by 85 automated unit, API, and frontend tests plus six live User Acceptance Tests.

\subsection{In-Distribution Discrimination, Calibration, and Subgroup Failure Profile}
\label{subsec:indist}

On the held-out ArtiFact test partition ($n = 7{,}505$: $3{,}779$ synthetic and $3{,}726$ authentic faces), the baseline ResNet-50 achieves ROC-AUC $0.9261$, area under the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC) $0.9348$, balanced accuracy $0.8483$, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) $0.6978$, Brier score $0.1131$, ECE $0.0525$, and maximum calibration error (MCE) $0.1544$. At the validation-frozen threshold $t = 0.3951$, the classifier misses 691 synthetic faces ($\text{FNR} = 18.29\%$) and falsely flags 449 authentic faces ($\text{FPR} = 12.05\%$). Figure~\ref{fig:calibration} illustrates the 15-bin calibration profile on this test split.

\begin{figure}[t]

Examining Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}(a) alongside the bimodal probability histogram in Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}(b) reveals an important forensic nuance: although overall ECE ($0.0525$) is low because most predictions concentrate in the extreme $0.0$ and $1.0$ bins (Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}(b)), intermediate bins exhibit substantial miscalibration ($\text{MCE} = 0.1544$), where observed fake proportions diverge noticeably from raw predicted probabilities. This empirical discrepancy directly justifies M9, which replaces raw $p$ with empirical validation-bin lookup $A_{\text{val}}(r)$ before computing decision confidence. Even more revealing is the subgroup error decomposition across individual seen generators and authentic sources shown in Figure~\ref{fig:subgroups}.

As Figure~\ref{fig:subgroups} demonstrates, an aggregate $18.29\%$ FNR conceals extreme intra-dataset heterogeneity. Legacy and lower-fidelity generators such as StarGAN and CIPS incur $0.0\%$ FNR, whereas modern alias-free and perceptual GANs suffer severe miss rates even though their generator families were present during training: $43.7\%$ FNR on StyleGAN3, $42.1\%$ on Projected GAN, $38.3\%$ on StyleGAN2, and $38.2\%$ on Mask-Aware Transformer (MAT). Simultaneously, authentic portraits from CelebA-HQ ($7.0\%$ FPR) and FFHQ ($14.6\%$ FPR) incur moderate error rates, whereas scanned artistic portraits from MetFaces trigger a $36.7\%$ FPR because non-photographic brush and scan textures deviate from studio camera statistics \cite{Wu_2026}.

\subsection{Perturbation Robustness Across Fifty-Three Image Degradations}
\label{subsec:robustness}

In operational investigations, facial images rarely arrive as uncompressed studio crops; they routinely undergo resizing, filtering, and lossy re-encoding across messaging and social platforms \cite{Guo_2024}. Figure~\ref{fig:robustness} plots balanced accuracy and ROC-AUC trajectories across all 53 synthetic degradation conditions spanning 15 perturbation families ($n = 7{,}505$ test images per condition).

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/FIG0016_v1.pdf}
  \caption{Balanced accuracy and ROC-AUC trajectories across fifteen perturbation families (a) through (o) on the ArtiFact test split.}
  \label{fig:robustness}
\end{figure*}

Inspecting the fifteen panels Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}(a) through Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}(o) separates detector sensitivity into two distinct regimes. First, moderate photometric and geometric adjustments, including brightness reduction and elevation (Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}(a), \ref{fig:robustness}(b)), contrast reduction and elevation (Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}(d), \ref{fig:robustness}(e)), desaturation and oversaturation (Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}(f), \ref{fig:robustness}(l)), mild occlusion (Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}(k)), and small in-plane rotations (Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}(n)), reduce balanced accuracy by less than $0.10$ while preserving ROC-AUC near $0.90$. Second, fine-detail and high-frequency degradations induce catastrophic failure: Gaussian blur (Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}(g)), additive Gaussian noise (Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}(h)), heavy JPEG compression down to quality factor 10 (Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}(i)), compound low-quality social-media uploads (Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}(j)), and aggressive spatial downscaling (Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}(m)) drive balanced accuracy down toward the $0.50$ chance floor. Importantly, comparing threshold-free ROC-AUC against fixed-threshold balanced accuracy in Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}(c) (center crop), Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}(i) (moderate JPEG), and Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}(o) (upscaling) reveals substantial operating-point drift: even when ranking separability partially survives, distortion shifts raw $P(\text{fake})$ upward on authentic faces until nearly every degraded real image is falsely accused as synthetic ($\text{FPR} \rightarrow 1.0$). Within our forensic architecture, M2 quality checks and M3 compression/noise/FFT reliability grading directly intercept degraded inputs so M6 and M9 cap decision confidence rather than trusting shifted neural logits \cite{Shen_2026}.

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Generalization and Controlled Unseen-Generator Holdout}
\label{subsec:cross}

Evaluating the baseline ResNet-50 zero-shot at its frozen threshold ($t = 0.3951$) across the four external benchmarks exposes both directions of cross-domain failure, plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:cross_dataset}.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/FIG0018_v1.pdf}
  \caption{Cross-dataset false negative and false positive rates of the baseline ResNet-50 across ArtiFact and four external benchmarks.}
  \label{fig:cross_dataset}
\end{figure}

On the synthetic-detection axis in Figure~\ref{fig:cross_dataset}, familiar StyleGAN portraits in 140k Real and Fake Faces incur only $9.2\%$ FNR because StyleGAN spectral footprints closely resemble ArtiFact training fakes. By contrast, across 17 unseen generator families, overall FNR jumps from $18.3\%$ in-distribution to $48.1\%$, reaching $50.2\%$ FNR on unseen diffusion faces in DiFF (and climbing to $81.5\%$ FNR on the FreeDoM\_I subset \cite{Cheng_2024}). Even more alarming from a judicial standpoint is the authentic-image false-alarm rate: external real faces suffer $30.4\%$ FPR on 140k FFHQ JPEGs, $76.7\%$ FPR on Synthbuster RAISE photographs (yielding near-chance overall Synthbuster ROC-AUC of $0.5172$ alongside $23.7\%$ diffusion FNR), and $82.6\%$ FPR on FairFace across all seven demographic subgroups. Consistent with the attribute-shortcut findings of Xu et al.\ \cite{Xu_2024}, because ArtiFact authentic images are dominated by high-resolution aligned portraits, the standalone CNN misinterprets legitimate in-the-wild lighting, skin-tone diversity, and native JPEG histories in FairFace as synthetic anomalies.

Because external benchmarks simultaneously vary generator architecture and camera acquisition pipeline, Figure~\ref{fig:unseen_logo} isolates pure unseen-generator shift on the identical ArtiFact acquisition pipeline via controlled Stable Diffusion holdout.

As Figure~\ref{fig:unseen_logo}(a) shows, the LOGO ResNet-50 maintains $83.3\%$ recall across its 12 seen generators and $88.6\%$ specificity on real faces (validation ROC-AUC $0.937$), proving that the model learned seen distributions effectively. Figure~\ref{fig:unseen_logo}(b) sharpens this comparison on the exact 299 Stable Diffusion test images shared by both splits: the baseline ResNet-50 (which saw Stable Diffusion during training) detects $96.3\%$, whereas the LOGO model (unseen) detects only $13.7\%$. This $82.6$-percentage-point paired collapse confirms that supervised spatial features learned from GANs and other generators do not automatically generalize to an unseen latent diffusion family \cite{Yan_2026}, justifying why the standalone ResNet-50 is unsuitable for autonomous deployment.

\subsection{Comparative Forensic Synthesis and Operational Implications}
\label{subsec:synthesis}

Situating our empirical and architectural results alongside the recent literature highlights why calibration and system governance must complement backbone research. Prior uncertainty-focused detectors such as PE-DD \cite{Jin_2025} reduce Brier score and ECE inside CNN ensembles yet still emit binary outputs that degrade under severe compression, while OOD abnormality modules \cite{Maiano_2026} struggle when transformer attention maps flatten across generator groups. Similarly, reliability-gated operator fusion in PORI-Gate \cite{Shen_2026} and fairness audits on DiffSynFace \cite{Panda_2026} both reveal that strong ranking AUC/AP frequently coexists with severe fixed-threshold accuracy collapse under score shift, mirroring the exact phenomenon we observed across all three backbones (Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}(c), \ref{fig:robustness}(i); Figure~\ref{fig:backbones}(b)). Whereas raising a single probability threshold to 80\%--85\% in judicial screening collapses recall to 18\% \cite{Hydara_2024}, decoupling M4 perception from M6 reliability grading, M7 cross-paradigm consensus, M9 four-dimensional calibrated confidence, and M8 four-way abstention (\textit{Real}, \textit{Fake}, \textit{Inconclusive}, \textit{Unknown}) routes ambiguous, degraded, or contested cases to human forensic review \cite{Silva_2022} without sacrificing verified provenance.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

In accordance with transparent forensic reporting, six scope boundaries of this study warrant explicit discussion. First, while our GPU comparator matrix spans three distinct neural paradigms (ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B4, and ViT-B/16), all three were evaluated under a fixed $224\times 224$ input contract to preserve strict grouped-split fairness; evaluating higher native resolutions ($299$\,px Xception \cite{Altamimi_2026}, $380$\,px EfficientNet-B4 \cite{Silva_2022}), space-frequency networks \cite{Guo_2024}, and vision-language encoders \cite{Wu_2026} remains scheduled for Version 2, and no multi-seed superiority is claimed. Second, two baseline seeds established run-to-run repeatability, whereas each ablation and backbone comparator ran for one seed, precluding multi-seed Wilcoxon signed-rank claims, and the from-scratch run reflects a fixed 11-epoch single-factor budget rather than an extended convergence schedule.

Third, within Version 1 of the forensic core, the nine M3 signal collectors supply reliability-graded contextual corroboration while M4 specialists ($K = 1$ or $K = 3$) cast directional votes under interpretable \texttt{rule\_based} fusion; training calibrated directional spectral \cite{Yan_2026}, operator-probe \cite{Shen_2026}, and biometric \cite{Xue_2023} specialists alongside learned \texttt{weighted} and \texttt{bayesian} fusion requires multi-collector ground-truth corpora in Version 2. Fourth, controlled LOGO evaluation held out Stable Diffusion only, and synthetic perturbations were applied to $224\times 224$ preprocessed crops rather than raw pre-crop camera uploads. Fifth, M10 currently enforces the structured explainability schema and honestly records \texttt{"no\_data"} when live Grad-CAM, LIME \cite{Reza_2026}, or pixel-fakeness tensors \cite{Huang_2022} are uncomputed at runtime. Finally, Version 1 targets single-face static images in a local deployment profile; temporal video, audio, and multimodal extensions registered via M18 \cite{Croitoru_2026}, production authentication hardening, and certified forensic-examiner usability trials \cite{Hydara_2024} represent direct avenues for future work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study demonstrated that reliable synthetic face authentication requires moving beyond standalone binary neural classification toward calibrated, auditable, and abstention-capable multi-specialist forensic systems. Across a strictly public five-dataset benchmark and eight controlled GPU experiments spanning ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B4, and Vision Transformer ViT-B/16, we proved that even scaling in-distribution performance from $0.9261$ ROC-AUC (ResNet-50) to $0.9750$ ROC-AUC and $0.0211$ ECE (ViT-B/16) fails to prevent $64.0\%$ unseen DiFF diffusion misses, $82.1\%$ Synthbuster misses, and $62.5\%$ external FairFace false accusations. By designing and verifying the eighteen-module AI Forensic Analyst (M1--M18) across 16 fault scenarios (135 ledger-verified sentences, zero ungrounded claims) and a $2{,}700$-case nine-track governance benchmark, we showed that combining physical integrity validation (M3/M6), cross-paradigm multi-specialist fusion (M7), validation-calibrated four-dimensional confidence (M9), and image-blind four-way decisioning (M8) reduces silent erroneous verdicts to $1.3\%$--$3.0\%$ on ArtiFact, 140k Faces, and Synthbuster, cuts FairFace false accusations by $72.1\%$, reduces unseen DiFF wrong verdicts by $32.4\%$--$43.8\%$, and eliminates $48.7\%$--$100.0\%$ of silent degradation errors. Future work will integrate foundation-model and dual-frequency directional specialists, learned Bayesian fusion, runtime localization maps, and multimodal audio-visual verification via M18.
